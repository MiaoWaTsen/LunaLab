Surface Age from Crater Counts

A fresh lava plain starts with no craters and collects them at a known rate. Count them, and you can read off how long it has been exposed — the way ages are assigned to almost every surface in the Solar System that no sample has come from.

📏 Reading the age back

Count today's craters in a size range and ask which age makes that count most likely (a Poisson likelihood). The answer depends on the smallest crater counted: on an old surface the small craters have saturated — new ones destroy as many as they add — so they stop recording time.

🧪 Real test: the Chang'e-6 landing site

Chang'e-6 returned basalts from the far side in 2024, dated in the laboratory to 2.807 billion years. Those samples played no part in calibrating the chronology, so this is an independent test. The craters are an expert count of 52 km² around the landing site, down to 30 m.

🗺️ Seven landing sites: where you count matters

The same method on every sampled mare site, with the global crater catalogue (Robbins 2018, complete to about 1 km). A crater count only dates one lava flow if it is made on that flow. Compare counting every crater in a circle with counting only on the mapped geological unit the site sits on (Unified Geologic Map of the Moon, 1:5M).

👻 Why young lavas still read old: ghost craters

A thin lava flow buries the craters beneath it only partly: kilometre-sized ones survive as "ghost" rings and get counted with the flow's own craters. The tell-tale sign: the age climbs as only larger craters are counted, because the larger the crater, the likelier it predates the lava. Dating young flows takes sub-kilometre craters, counted on high-resolution images — like the Chang'e-6 count above.

🧮 Method and data

Production function N(≥D) ∝ 10^(Σ aₖ (log D)ᵏ), 11th-degree polynomial (Neukum et al. 2001). Chronology N(≥1 km) = a(e^(bT) − 1) + cT: Neukum et al. (2001), and Yue et al. (2022) recalibrated with Chang'e-5.

Age inference: with the size distribution fixed, the number of craters in the fitted range is all the data says about the age; its Poisson likelihood over a 1 Myr age grid gives the posterior (Michael et al. 2016). A calibration test checks that the 90% interval holds the true age of synthetic surfaces ~90% of the time.
